Framing

What Claude is, and what it isn't

Is
A thinking partner for analysis, writing, and iteration
A drafting tool that turns blank pages into starting points
A research assistant, paired with verification
Isn't
A search engine — it doesn't browse the web by default
An oracle — it can be confidently wrong
A vault — assume nothing you paste in is private

You cannot break Claude. Every chat is independent. If something feels off, close it and start over.

◆TakeawayTreat Claude as a sharp drafting partner — never a private or infallible oracle.

Caution

What to watch for

1
Hallucinations
Claude can invent facts, citations, and regulations that sound credible. Always verify anything that matters, especially numbers, dates, and compliance details.
2
Source mixing
Give Claude two documents and it may blend them into one answer without telling you. Be specific about what comes from where, or work one source at a time.
3
Sensitive data
Never paste student PII, personnel records, or confidential financials. If you wouldn't email it to a vendor, don't paste it into Claude.
4
Use judgment
Claude is a co-pilot, not autopilot. You are still responsible for what goes out the door. Treat every output as a draft, not a final answer.
◆TakeawayEvery output is a draft. You stay responsible for what goes out the door.

Framework · Remember This One

RCTF: the recipe for prompts that work

R
Role
Who you want Claude to be
C
Context
What Claude needs to know
T
Task
Exactly what you want done
F
Format
How the output should look
Hit all four and your output quality jumps immediately.
Skip one and Claude has to guess.
◆TakeawayRole, Context, Task, Format. Hit all four and output quality jumps immediately.

Part 1 · Prompting
RCTF · Example

Lazy prompt vs. RCTF prompt

Lazy Prompt
"Here is our updated expense policy. Write an email to our principals about it."
Claude writes a long, formal letter nobody will read — and guesses at everything else.
RCTF Prompt
RoleYou are part of the EMAN central office writing to our school principals.
Context📎 [expense-policy-update.pdf attached] — We are replacing email-based approvals with the emanApprove Portal. Threshold changes from $150 to $300. Effective July 1.
TaskAnnounce this change. They need to know what's different, when it starts, and what to do on day one.
Format5 sentences max. Two bullet points for the key changes. One clear action item at the end. Warm but direct. No corporate language.
◆TakeawayFormat is the most skipped line — and it's what most controls whether the output is actually usable.

Part 2 · Prompting
Technique · High Leverage

Reverse prompting

The magic phrase — tell Claude to ask you questions first
"Before you answer, ask me any clarifying questions you need to make your output as useful as possible."
Why it works
The output is only as good as the input. Claude is better than you at writing prompts to itself, so let it.
When to use it
Any task with constraints, dependencies, or decisions. Scheduling, planning, drafting strategy, hiring criteria.
◆TakeawayLet Claude interview you first. It writes better prompts to itself than you do.

Part 1 · The Golden Rule
Use Your Judgment

Claude drafts. You decide.

Claude hands you a strong first draft — not a finished, final answer. It can sound completely sure and still be wrong.

Never paste an output straight into an email and hit send.
Before anything goes out
1

Read it critically. Does the logic actually hold up?

2

Validate every source. Can you trace each claim and number back to where it came from?

3

Don't forget your audience. Easy to digest, and only the details they care about — the right KPIs to the right person.

4

Tweak it until it says what you mean and sounds like you.

◆TakeawayYou own every word that leaves your desk. Review it, verify it, and make it yours.

So… what is this thing?

Tools like Claude are generative AI — you type a request in plain English, and it writes back. Think of it as an exceptionally well-read assistant that can draft, explain, summarize, and think through problems with you. No coding, no special skills. You just talk to it.

You ask in plain words
Type a question or request the same way you'd message a colleague.
It writes back in seconds
A draft, a summary, an answer — ready for you to react to and refine.
You go back and forth
Tell it what to change. It adjusts. You stay in control the whole time.

Part 2 · Technique
Technique · Catch Your Own Mistakes

Make Claude audit itself

Give it a role, then tell it to check its own work
"You are an expert-level detail catcher. Before you finalize, review your own draft for errors, weak spots, and anything unclear or unsupported — then fix them and tell me what you changed."
Why it works
A role sharpens the lens, and a second pass catches what the first draft missed. You get a cleaner draft before you even start reading.
When to use it
Anything that matters — board docs, parent letters, anything going out the door. Stack it with the safety check from Part 1.
◆TakeawayGive Claude a role and a second pass. It catches its own mistakes before you have to.

Technique · Keep Going

Don't stop at the first answer

Claude's first answer is a starting point, not the final word. It remembers the whole conversation — so just keep steering until it's right.
◆  Just say things like…
›"Make it shorter."
›"More formal — it's going to the board."
›"Give me three versions."
›"You missed the deadline — add it."
›"Explain it like I'm not a finance person."
›"Now turn it into an email."
◆TakeawayThe magic isn't the first answer — it's the back-and-forth.

Live Demo · From Survey to Schedule

400 students. 100 seats. One week.

Our testing center seats 50, with two 3-hour sessions a day, Monday to Friday. We need to test ~400 students — and they travel from across the state, so many can only come on certain days. So we sent a survey.

Download the survey · 400 responses
Attach the survey, then paste:
The Prompt
"Build a testing schedule from this survey. Center seats 50 per session, two 3-hour sessions a day, Mon–Fri. First, ask me any clarifying questions. Then seat all ~400 students on days they're available, never over 50 a session, flag anyone who can't be placed, and give me a clean schedule I can use to prep the rooms."
It asks a couple of questions first, then reads all 400 rows, respects every constraint at once, and hands back a clean day-by-day schedule — with the headcount for each block.
